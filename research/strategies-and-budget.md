# Purchase strategies and budget

Updated: 2026-10-25. Illustrations, not approvals or property valuations.

## Scenarios

Assumptions: $250,000 down; 25-year amortization; 4.75% nominal annual interest compounded semi-annually; monthly payments. Closing costs are funded separately. The rate is a planning input, not a quote. See the source register for advertised-rate references.

| Purchase price | Mortgage principal | Monthly payment | Gross monthly rent needed for $5,000 total* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $850,000 | $600,000 | $3,405 | $0 |
| $1,000,000 | $750,000 | $4,256 | $1,429 |
| $1,100,000 | $850,000 | $4,823 | $2,647 |
| $1,200,000 | $950,000 | $5,391 | $3,865 |
| $1,250,000 | $1,000,000 | $5,675 | $4,474 |

*Counts 50% of gross rent (the ownership-model offset) against a representative townhouse allowance: strata $400/month, tax $3,500/year, insurance $150/month, utilities $200/month, maintenance 0.5% of price/year. This arbitrary screening allowance is not a tax calculation, an estimate of actual expenses, or a lender's qualification method. Rent figures above are REQUIRED rents, not researched local achievable rents. Income tax can reduce the benefit further. Replace the allowance with an itemized property-specific budget.

Major repairs and transaction costs are additional; the other items are now included in the total above. At $1.25M the down payment is exactly 20%; above that price, financing/insurance assumptions need revision.

Earlier $850,000–$880,000 mortgage-only ceiling was an approximate 4.5%–5% / 25-year illustration, not a fixed affordability limit. On 2026-10-25 the $3,500 mortgage-only target was replaced by a $5,000 total monthly payment target. A 30-year alternative has not yet been compared.

## Qualification versus comfort

Gross household income is about $15,000/month. A 39% screening ratio is $5,850/month for qualifying housing costs. This is a reference, not a universal uninsured-lender cap. At an illustrative 6.75% stress-test rate, a $1.1M purchase has about $5,823/month in qualifying mortgage payments before property tax and heat; rental-income treatment therefore matters. Lender review must establish accepted income, rent, credit, taxes, heat, and applicable debt-service rules.

Initial research bands proposed by the assistant, not selected by the user:

- Without rent: roughly $750,000–$850,000. Under the $5,000 total target this band still holds: an $850,000 townhouse totals about $4,800/month in the model.
- With a suite: investigate roughly $1.0M–$1.15M; test $1.2M only with verified rents and acceptable reserves. Under the $5,000 total target the suite branch needs roughly $1,400–$3,300/month verified gross rent (at the 50% offset) — far above the $1,250–$1,650 modelled for the current suite examples; see [rent vs buy](rent-vs-buy.md).

## Renting instead

See [rent vs buy](rent-vs-buy.md): renting an apartment at $2,700/month versus buying one of these homes, $250,000 down at 4.75% / 25 years, home values growing 2% a year, the $250,000 and the monthly surplus invested at 5% a year if renting, 5% selling costs on exit. At those assumptions renting leaves the household wealthier for 43 of the 43 homes with a known total at 5 years; only the cheapest home (P23, $479,900) breaks even by year 10. Most townhouses need 3.6–4.3% annual price growth to break even over 5 years. Buying in this sample is a lifestyle and stability decision — a yard, a school catchment, control over the home — not the wealth-maximizing one at 2% appreciation.

## Strategy comparison

See [community discussions and interviews](community-strategies.md) for additional branches: co-buying, cohousing (including Langley), living in the smaller unit, leaseholds, equity/nonprofit co-ops, rentvesting, homestay and rent-to-own. These are exploratory; none has been selected or property-screened.

| Strategy | Potential benefit | Key unresolved checks |
|---|---|---|
| Resale townhouse with private yard | Lower debt; no tenant required | School pair; yard rights; strata fees, minutes, insurance, reserve fund, depreciation report and levies |
| Detached with existing permitted suite | Yard/land and rental support | Municipal permits and occupancy; achievable rent; tenancy; insurance; taxes; privacy, sound and yard arrangements |
| Older detached farther out | Space and possible land value | Commute; school pathway; roof, drainage, structure and systems; flood exposure |
| Duplex/small strata | Alternative between townhouse and detached | Title, bylaws, shared obligations, insurance and suite permissions |
| Buy and convert/add a suite | Potential customization | Not yet costed; permits, feasibility, construction financing, delays and contingency; do not assume future rent |
| Continue renting while preparing | Flexibility; opportunity to test location | Current rent, savings/investment plan and school continuity; ownership is not automatically the best financial outcome |

## New versus resale

Working preference for comparison: resale and completed new homes first, not a final recommendation.

- New: inspect build quality and builder history; confirm warranty coverage; calculate GST and any eligible rebate, property transfer tax relief, landscaping and extras. Presales add delivery and financing uncertainty.
- Resale: assess actual condition, neighbourhood and usable yard; review inspection, permits and maintenance history. For strata, evaluate funding and projected common repairs.
- Tax exemptions and rebates depend on purchaser and transaction eligibility; first-time buyer status remains unknown.

## Required property-level model

Record purchase/closing cash; actual mortgage quote and renewal sensitivity; gross and after-tax rent; vacancy/nonpayment; utilities and insurance changes; routine and capital repairs; strata obligations; taxes; total monthly outlay; and a no-rent reserve scenario. Include selling costs and holding period when comparing long-term value.
